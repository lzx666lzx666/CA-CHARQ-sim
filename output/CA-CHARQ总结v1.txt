CA-CHARQ: Confidence-Adaptive Cooperative HARQ for Multi-Hop UASNs
—— Protocol Design, System Model, and Simulation Results
Date: 2026-06-09 | Version: v11

1. Introduction
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1.1 Background and Motivation

Underwater acoustic sensor networks (UASNs) face unique physical-layer
constraints absent from terrestrial wireless: propagation speed of 1500 m/s
(5 orders of magnitude slower than radio), frequency-dependent absorption
(Thorp's formula, α≈4.1 dB/km at 20 kHz), and severe multi-path without
a stable line-of-sight component (Rayleigh fading, K=0). For a 5-hop
linear network with 600 m hop distance, a single retransmission round-trip
consumes ≈14 s in acoustic propagation alone, making every additional
transmission a significant cost in latency and energy.

Cooperative HARQ (C-HARQ) protocols attempt to mitigate these costs by
exploiting relay nodes as alternate data sources. Ghosh et al. [1] proposed
using RCPC codes to generate fixed-size FEC packets (Pac-1=50 blocks,
Pac-2=40 blocks) from cooperative nodes, which are transmitted sequentially
upon negative acknowledgement. While this improves over non-cooperative
schemes, the fixed FEC size creates a structural inefficiency: under
favorable channel conditions the receiver may need only a fraction of
the 90-block redundancy, yet the protocol forces full transmission.

The key gap across prior work is the absence of a closed-loop feedback
mechanism that quantifies the destination's decoding margin and adapts
the helper's redundancy accordingly. All existing protocols use fixed
redundancy sizes independent of instantaneous channel quality.

1.3 Contributions

This work presents CA-CHARQ (Confidence-Adaptive Cooperative HARQ), with
the following contributions:

(1) Confidence Quantization (Cpkt). The destination computes a normalized
    decoding margin δ = SNR_eff − SNR_50 from accumulated soft information,
    quantizes it into a 2-bit Cpkt value (0–3), and piggybacks it on the
    NACK packet. This creates a low-overhead feedback channel from the
    receiver to both source and helpers.

(2) Bottleneck-Distance Contention. Multiple helpers compete via a
    weighted backoff timer that accounts for each helper's self-confidence,
    residual energy, and the larger of its distances to source and
    destination. A layered fallback mechanism handles the low-SNR regime
    where competition overhead outweighs its benefit.

(3) Grace Period. When Cpkt ≥ 3 (high confidence), the source suspends
    immediate retransmission and waits for a helper-originated ACK. If
    the helper succeeds, one source retransmission (100 blocks) is saved.

(4) Adaptive Redundancy. The winning helper selects its RV based on Cpkt:
    RV1 = 30 parity blocks (Cpkt≥2), RV2 = 60 (Cpkt=1), RV3 = 90 (Cpkt=0).

(5) L2S PHY Abstraction. A Shannon-capacity-based link-to-system interface
    replaces actual codec instances, ensuring fair protocol comparison
    while enabling extensive parameter-space scanning (SNR/Distance/Helper
    count) at practical computational cost.

(6) Differentiated Helper Power. Helper transmit power scales as
    (d_dest/600)^1.5, reflecting the shorter path loss to the destination
    and improving energy efficiency.


2. System Model
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
2.1 Network Topology

We consider a 5-hop linear UASN. The source node (node 0) transmits data
packets hop-by-hop through relay nodes 1–4 to the destination (node 5).
The hop distance is d_hop = 600 m. At each hop, Nh = 3 helper nodes are
randomly deployed within the intersecting region of two circles of radius
d_hop centered at the transmitter and receiver respectively.

Nodes operate in half-duplex mode. We assume that medium access control
issues have been resolved, following the convention of [1] and [5].
The data packet arrival process at the source follows an exponential
distribution with mean 30 s, modeling sparse underwater sensing traffic.

2.2 Underwater Acoustic Channel

The transmission loss between two nodes separated by distance d (in meters)
at acoustic frequency f (in kHz) is:

    TL(d, f) = (d/1000)^1.5 × 10^(α(f)·d/10000)                    (1)

where α(f) is the Thorp absorption coefficient [6]:

    α(f) = 0.11f²/(1+f²) + 44f²/(4100+f²) + 2.75×10⁻⁴f² + 0.003  (2)

At the operating frequency f = 20 kHz, α ≈ 4.1 dB/km. The cylindrical
spreading exponent 1.5 is used for practical shallow-water propagation [1].

The channel is modeled as block-Rayleigh fading (K = 0). Each chunk
experiences an independent fading coefficient |h|² = (I²+Q²), where
I ∼ N(0, 1/2) and Q ∼ N(0, 1/2) are i.i.d. Gaussian. The instantaneous
SNR on chunk i at the receiver is:

    γ_i = (P_tx / TL(d,f)) × |h_i|² / N_0_W                          (3)

where P_tx is the transmit power of the sending node and N_0_W is the
equivalent AWGN noise power spectral density. For a target per-hop average
SNR γ_target (dB), N_0_W is calibrated as:

    N_0_W = P_tx / (TL(600 m, 20 kHz) × 10^(γ_target/10))            (4)

2.3 Physical-Layer Decoding Model

Each receiving node maintains a 190-dimensional soft_buffer vector per
active packet ID. Element i stores the accumulated SNR from all received
copies of chunk i, implementing maximal-ratio combining (MRC) for repeated
system chunks (Chase combining) and soft combining for newly received
parity chunks (incremental redundancy, IR). The 190-element dimension
corresponds to the maximum possible size of an RCPC mother codeword
(100 systematic + 90 parity chunks).

The decoding outcome is determined by a two-layer link-to-system (L2S)
interface without instantiating actual channel codecs.

Layer 1 — Shannon Mutual Information Mapping:
    mi_i = log₂(1 + γ_i)                        for i ∈ active chunks
    MI_avg = (1/N_a) Σ mi_i                                          (5)
    γ_eff = 2^(MI_avg) − 1          [linear]                        (6)

where N_a = |{i : γ_i > 0}| is the number of active chunk positions.

Layer 2 — Code-Rate-Dependent Sigmoid BLER:
    R = 100 / N_a                  [effective code rate]            (7)
    γ_shannon = 2^R − 1            [Shannon limit, linear]          (8)
    gap(R) = 2.5 − R               [practical coding gap, dB]       (9)
    γ_50 = γ_shannon + gap(R)      [50th-percentile SNR, dB]       (10)
    BLER = [1 + exp(κ·(γ_eff,dB − γ_50))]^{-1}  with κ = 4.0      (11)

The decoding succeeds if and only if r ∼ Uniform(0,1) > BLER, where r is
a pseudo-random sample drawn independently at each decoding attempt.
Equations (5)–(6) capture the benefit of Chase combining (γ_i increases
for repeatedly received chunks → MI_avg increases → γ_eff increases).
Equations (7)–(10) capture the benefit of IR (new parity chunks increase
N_a → R decreases → γ_50 shifts leftward). Both mechanisms reduce BLER
through independent terms in the composite mapping.

2.4 Resource Units and Transmission Time

A chunk is the elementary soft-information unit, consisting of an
80-bit payload that experiences a single independent fading realization.
The data rate is R_b = 1200 bps, giving a chunk transmission time of
T_chunk = 80 / 1200 = 0.067 s.

Table 2. Redundancy version (RV) partitioning.
┌───────┬─────────────┬──────┬──────────┬──────────────────────────┐
│ RV    │ Chunk Range │ Size │ Tx Time  │ Used By                  │
├───────┼─────────────┼──────┼──────────┼──────────────────────────┤
│ RV0   │ [0, 100)    │ 100  │ 6.67 s   │ All protocols (source)   │
│ RV1   │ [100, 130)  │ 30   │ 2.00 s   │ CA-CHARQ helper (Cpkt≥2) │
│ RV2   │ [100, 160)  │ 60   │ 4.00 s   │ CA-CHARQ helper (Cpkt=1) │
│ RV3   │ [100, 190)  │ 90   │ 6.00 s   │ CA-CHARQ helper (Cpkt=0) │
│ Pac-1 │ [100, 150)  │ 50   │ 3.33 s   │ C-HARQ helper            │

└───────┴─────────────┴──────┴──────────┴──────────────────────────┘

RV0 contains the 100 systematic data chunks, always transmitted by the
source. RV1–RV3 contain increasing amounts of parity chunks (30/60/90)
and are selected adaptively by the CA-CHARQ helper based on Cpkt.
Pac-1 and Pac-2 are the fixed-size FEC packets of C-HARQ, together
spanning the same 90-chunk parity range as RV3.

2.5 Energy Consumption Model

Only transmit energy is accounted for, as underwater acoustic transmission
dominates the energy budget (transmit power ≈ 20× receive power [1]).
The source nodes transmit at P_tx = 15 W. For CA-CHARQ helpers, the
transmit power is scaled by the relative path loss advantage:

    P_h = P_tx × (d_dest / d_hop)^1.5                                 (12)

where d_dest is the Euclidean distance from the helper to the destination
node in its assigned hop. A helper at mid-point (d_dest ≈ 300 m) thus
transmits at approximately 35% of the source power, reflecting its
shorter range to the destination.

2.6 Performance Metrics

End-to-End Delay (D): time elapsed from packet creation at the source
(env.now at tx_queue.put) until successful delivery acknowledgment at
the final destination (stats.e2e_success), averaged over all completed
packets.

Transmission Overhead (η): total number of transmitted chunks across all
nodes divided by the total number of successfully delivered system chunks.

    η = Σ(num_chunks of all transmissions) / (N_success × 100)       (13)

Energy Efficiency (ξ): number of successfully delivered information bits
divided by total transmit energy consumption.

    ξ = (N_success × 100 × 80) / Σ(P_tx_node × T_tx)    [bits/Joule] (14)

Successful Packet Count (N_success): number of packets completing all
5 hops within the simulation time.


3. Protocol Design
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
3.1 CA-CHARQ Overview

CA-CHARQ operates in three per-hop phases:

Phase 1 — Source Transmission. The source transmits RV0 (100 systematic
chunks). The destination accumulates these chunks into its soft_buffer
positions [0, 100). All helpers in the overlapping region overhear
the broadcast and independently attempt soft-decoding.

Phase 2 — Confidence Feedback. The destination computes δ = SNR_eff − SNR_50
from its accumulated soft_buffer via Eqs. (5)–(10), quantizes δ to Cpkt
∈ {0,1,2,3}, and broadcasts a NACK packet carrying Cpkt. The source
receives this NACK directly. All helpers that have successfully decoded
the source's RV0 overhear the NACK.

Phase 3a — Grace Period (Cpkt ≥ 3). The source does not immediately
retransmit RV0. Instead, it starts a timer of duration Grace_t (see §3.4)
and waits for a helper-originated ACK. Meanwhile, all decoded helpers
enter the contention phase (§3.3). If any helper's parity transmission
enables the destination to decode successfully, the destination sends
ACK. Upon receiving ACK during Grace_t, the source marks the hop as
successful without transmitting a retry.

Phase 3b — Immediate Retry (Cpkt < 3). The source retransmits RV0
immediately. If Cpkt ≤ 1, the contention mechanism is bypassed and only
the pre-selected bottleneck-optimal helper transmits parity directly
(see §3.5). If Cpkt = 2, full competition among decoded helpers takes
place.

3.2 Confidence Quantization

The normalized decoding margin δ = γ_eff(dB) − γ_50(dB) is computed from
Eqs. (5)–(10). The Cpkt quantization thresholds are:

    δ > −0.2  → Cpkt = 3  (high confidence: BLER < 2%)
    δ > −2.0  → Cpkt = 2  (medium confidence)
    δ > −3.5  → Cpkt = 1  (low confidence)
    δ ≤ −3.5  → Cpkt = 0  (very low confidence)

Rationale: The threshold −0.2 dB for Cpkt = 3 corresponds to BLER ≈ 2%
on the sigmoid curve (Eq. 11, κ=4.0), which is the operational point
where a single RV1 transmission (30 parity chunks) is sufficient to
drive the residual BLER below 10⁻⁴. The threshold −3.5 dB for Cpkt = 0
corresponds to BLER ≈ 50%, beyond which helper decoding is unlikely.
These thresholds were calibrated against Ghosh's RCPC waterfall curves
and verified to produce consistent protocol differentiation across the
0–4 dB SNR range.

3.3 Helper Contention

Each decoded helper computes a contention score:

    d_bneck = max(d_src, d_dst)       [bottleneck distance, m]      (15)
    t_prop = d_bneck / 1500           [one-way propagation, s]      (16)
    S = w₁·min(c_pkt, 1.5) + w₂·(E_res / E_init)                  (17)
        + w₃ / (1 + t_prop / 0.4)

where c_pkt is the helper's own decoding confidence (continuous, from its
received RV0), E_res is its residual energy, and (w₁,w₂,w₃) = (0.40, 0.25,
0.35). The backoff timer is:

    t_backoff = (1 − clip(S, 0, 1)) × T_WIN × 2 + 0.05 s           (18)

with override rules:
    S > 0.90  → t_backoff = 0    (zero backoff for top-scoring helper)
    Cpkt < 3 ∧ is_pre_selected → t_backoff = 0.02 s (priority send)

The first helper whose timer expires begins transmitting its selected RV.
Other helpers overhearing this transmission trigger a cancel event on the
same packet ID and withdraw from contention.

Sensitivity of (w₁,w₂,w₃) to CA-CHARQ end-to-end delay was evaluated
across a 3×3 grid of (w₁,w₃) ∈ {0.2, 0.3, 0.4} × {0.2, 0.35, 0.5} with
w₂ = 1−w₁−w₃ (when feasible). Delay varies by less than ±4% across the
grid, indicating that CA-CHARQ performance is robust to weight variations
and is primarily driven by the structural design of contention rather than
precise weight tuning. (Detailed heatmap: output/AppendixA_weights.png)

3.4 Grace Period

When Cpkt ≥ 3, the source computes:

    Grace_t = rv_tx_t + T_WIN/4 + 3·t_prop + 0.3 s                (19)

where:
    rv_tx_t = expected helper transmit time, derived from Cpkt:
              Cpkt=3 → RV1 → 2.0 s;  Cpkt=2 → RV1 → 2.0 s
    T_WIN/4 ≈ 0.375 s = buffer for contention backoff of the fastest helper
    3·t_prop = 3 × 600/1500 = 1.2 s = worst-case ACK round-trip margin
    0.3 s = ACK processing and SimPy scheduling jitter

The source yields on the event pair [Grace_t timeout, helper_ACK_event].
If the ACK event fires first, the hop succeeds without source retry.

3.5 Low-SNR Fallback (v11 optimization)

When Cpkt ≤ 1 (very low confidence), the contention mechanism is
skipped entirely (contend() returns immediately). A single pre-selected
helper, chosen during topology initialization by minimizing d_bneck
across all three helpers, transmits RV2 (60 parity chunks) or RV3 (90)
directly. This eliminates the SimPy event scheduling overhead of 3-helper
competition (backoff timers, cancel events) at SNRs where the probability
of any helper decoding is low, improving the 0–0.5 dB overhead by 15%
compared to the full-competition baseline (v10).

The additional complexity of CA-CHARQ relative to C-HARQ is concentrated
in the Cpkt computation (3 additional arithmetic ops) and helper
contention (3 SimPy events per hop). Both are O(1) and negligible compared
to the acoustic propagation and transmission times.

3.7 Baseline Protocols

S&W ARQ: No cooperation. The source retransmits RV0 upon NACK. Chase
combining at the destination aggregates successive RV0 copies.

C-ARQ: One pre-selected helper (closest to the mid-point) retransmits
RV0 upon NACK. Both source and helper RV0s undergo Chase combining.

C-HARQ [1]: One pre-selected helper. Upon hearing NACK, it proactively
transmits Pac-1 (50 chunks) followed unconditionally by Pac-2 (40 chunks)
after a 3.53 s gap. The destination performs Chase combining on RV0 copies
and IR combining on Pac-1/Pac-2 chunks.

5. Results and Discussion
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
5.1 End-to-End Delay vs. SNR

Figure: output/v11_Delay_Overhead.png (left panel)

Observation 1. CA-CHARQ achieves the lowest E2E delay across 0–3.5 dB,
leading C-HARQ by 5–11% (76 s vs. 83 s at 0 dB, 55 s vs. 62 s at 2 dB).
The bottleneck-distance contention ensures the helper with the best
joint source-helper and helper-destination channel is selected.

Observation 2. Rayleigh fading (K=0) amplifies the cooperative gain.
The S&W ARQ delay rises steeply at 0 dB (93 s) because it relies solely
on source Chase combining without spatial diversity. CA-CHARQ's
multi-helper competition provides diversity against deep fades.

Observation 3. Above 3.5 dB, all protocols converge to ≈38 s — the
one-shot transmission floor, where the source's single RV0 suffices
for decoding and no retransmission is needed.

5.2 Transmission Overhead vs. SNR

Figure: output/v11_Delay_Overhead.png (right panel)

Observation 4. The v11 low-SNR fallback (§3.5) enables CA-CHARQ to
achieve lower overhead than both C-HARQ and C-ARQ at 0–0.5 dB for the
first time (12.72 vs. 13.90/14.24 at 0 dB). In v10 (full competition
at all Cpkt levels), the corresponding value was 15.06 — a 15%
improvement from selectively bypassing contention.

Observation 5. Cooperative protocols exhibit an arch-shaped overhead
profile relative to S&W: the ratio peaks at 1–2 dB (where source
retransmissions decrease but helper transmissions persist), then drops
sharply once Grace Period activates (Cpkt ≥ 3, ≥ 2.5 dB). CA-CHARQ's
drop from 12.67 (2.0 dB) to 9.93 (2.5 dB) represents a 21% reduction
driven by Grace-enabled source retry savings.

5.3 Energy Efficiency vs. SNR

Figure: output/v11_EE.png

Observation 6. S&W ARQ dominates EE below 3.0 dB due to zero helper
energy expenditure. CA-CHARQ overtakes S&W at 3.0 dB (12.2 vs. 11.8
bits/Joule). The differentiated helper power (Eq. 12) and Grace Period
jointly enable this cross-over: helpers use 35–70% of source power
because of their shorter range, while Grace saves the source 100 chunks
per successful helper intervention.

Observation 7. At 400 m (SNR≈+2.9 dB), all protocols deliver ≈260 packets

Observation 8. At 800 m (SNR≈−2.7 dB), CA-CHARQ delay (561 s) is 39%

Observation 9. At 1000 m (SNR≈−5.0 dB), CA-CHARQ delivers 124 successful
packets vs. 44 for C-HARQ (2.8×) and 27 for S&W (4.6×). The overhead is
27.58 vs. 82.05 for C-HARQ (66% reduction). This is the regime where
the compound benefit of adaptive RV selection, Grace Period, and helper
competition is maximized — the source would otherwise require 4–5 full
RV0 retransmissions per hop.

5.5 Helper Count Sweep

Figure: output/v11_Helpers_Delay.png (CA-CHARQ only)

Observation 10. CA-CHARQ E2E delay decreases monotonically from 82.4 s
(Nh=1) to 73.7 s (Nh=5), a 10.5% reduction. The largest marginal gain
occurs between Nh=1 and Nh=2 (5.8 s), after which diminishing returns
set in. This confirms that the contention mechanism effectively exploits
spatial diversity: a larger helper pool increases the probability of
selecting a node with favorable joint source-helper and helper-destination
channel conditions.

5.6 Discussion of Model Limitations

Channel model: Rayleigh block fading (K=0) captures the multi-path
scattering dominant in shallow-water UASNs but does not model frequency-
selective fading or Doppler spread from platform motion. The equivalent
AWGN noise calibration (Eq. 4) abstracts the four-component ambient noise
spectrum (turbulence, shipping, waves, thermal) into a single scalar.
These simplifications are consistent with the PHY abstraction level used
in [2-4].

L2S interface: The sigmoid-based BLER prediction (Eq. 11) replaces actual
channel decoding with a statistical mapping. While this abstracts
code-specific performance (error floors, decoding convergence speed),
the gap parameter 2.5−R was calibrated to match RCPC waterfall behavior
as reported in [1], and the relative protocol ranking is insensitive to
the specific mapping function as long as all protocols share the same
PHY interface.

MAC layer: Following the convention of [1] and [5], medium access control
is assumed resolved. The backoff timers in the CA-CHARQ contention phase
(§3.3) include a 0.05 s protection gap to emulate collision-avoidance
quiet periods, but a full CSMA/CA or TDMA MAC is not modeled.

Energy model: Only transmit energy is tracked. Receive and idle energy
(≈ 5% of transmit power, per WHOI modem specifications) is omitted, as
is the computational energy of Cpkt calculation (3 log₂+mean+exp
operations per decoding attempt, negligible compared to acoustic
transmission).


6. Conclusion
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
This work presented CA-CHARQ, a confidence-adaptive cooperative HARQ
protocol for multi-hop underwater acoustic sensor networks. The protocol
introduces a Cpkt quantization feedback loop that enables closed-loop
adaptation of helper redundancy, a bottleneck-distance-weighted contention
mechanism for helper selection, and a Grace Period for high-confidence
source retry avoidance.

Simulation results under Rayleigh fading at 20 kHz with Thorp absorption
(α≈4.1 dB/km) show that CA-CHARQ: (i) achieves 5–11% lower E2E delay than
C-HARQ across 0–3.5 dB SNR; (ii) reduces overhead below all cooperative
baselines at 0–0.5 dB through the low-SNR fallback design; (iii) delivers
2.8× more packets than C-HARQ at 1000 m with 66% lower overhead; and
(iv) demonstrates spatial diversity gain through monotonic delay reduction
with increasing helper count (82.4→73.7 s for Nh=1→5).

Future work includes developing an absorbing Markov chain model for the
multi-helper CA-CHARQ to derive closed-form PER expressions, extending the
protocol to three-dimensional random deployments, and integrating a
frequency-dependent ambient noise model for environment-specific
performance prediction.
